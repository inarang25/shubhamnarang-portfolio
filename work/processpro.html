The problem

Every company knows how its buying should work. Very few can see how it actually works.

A purchase order, a goods receipt, an invoice and a payment each look correct on their own. The problems hide in the order they happened in, in who did them, and in how far apart they were. That evidence is spread across thousands of ERP records that no one reads end to end.

An invoice dated before the order existed. A batch released from quarantine by the storekeeper instead of Quality. A ₹15 lakh need split into two orders that each stay under the approval limit. None of these show up in a single document.

Every document looked fine on its own. The problems only appear when you line them up in time.

The company

Aravelle Formulations, a fictional generic-medicines maker near Ahmedabad.

One GMP plant, 40 suppliers, 120 materials and 12 people across purchasing, stores, Quality and accounts. I configured the ERP like an implementation: approval at ₹10 lakh, goods into quarantine, Quality release before payment. Its ideal process has six steps.

  1. RequestA department records the need and owns the budget.
  2. OrderA buyer orders from an approved supplier at an agreed price.
  3. ApprovalOrders of ₹10 lakh or more need the Purchase Head.
  4. Into quarantineGoods arrive into a locked area. Nobody may use them yet.
  5. Quality release + invoiceThe lab releases the batch. The invoice arrives in parallel.
  6. PaymentOnly when the batch is released and the invoice is in.

What makes pharma different: Quality co-owns purchasing. Under Good Manufacturing Practice, only the Quality unit decides which supplier may supply which material, and when a batch may be used. Buying from an unapproved supplier or paying before release is a regulatory finding first and a cost problem second.

The approach

From raw ERP tables to a process, in five stages.

I built every layer myself, the way an implementation team and a process-mining team would split the work.

  1. Build the ERPApprovals, receipt into quarantine, batches with expiry, payment terms and 12 role-based users.Odoo 17 · PostgreSQL · Docker
  2. Run a year of buyingA planned year with 14 kinds of mistake planted at known rates, each written to an answer key.Python
  3. ExtractSQL over 23 tables turns documents into an event log: case, step, time, person.SQL · CSV · XES
  4. CheckAn editable process model checks the order of steps. Fourteen business rules check amounts, roles, dates and quality data. Each finding is valued in rupees.PM4Py · pandas
  5. ShowA six-page tool: overview, process explorer, conformance, issues, single order and the data behind every number.Streamlit · Graphviz

What I learned

Three insights from the build.

A process map can't see moneyA ₹69 lakh order with no approval follows a perfectly normal path. That is why ProcessPro pairs the process model with business rules: together they explain why 76.5% conform to the model but only 42% follow every rule.
Exact-match controls fail on human typingThe ERP blocked 12 duplicate invoices and missed 17 that were retyped, INV/25-26/0652 against INV-25-26-0652.
Mistakes cascadeAn invoice dated before its order starts the due-date clock early, so payment ends up late — with nobody planting it.
Three fixes close most of itMatch the order and delivery before posting an invoice; hold payment on price or quantity differences; let Quality block unapproved suppliers and unreleased batches.
How I know it's right

In a real company, accuracy means reconciliation.

Because the data is planted, I could grade every rule against the answer key, and check the tool's counts against the ERP itself.

13 / 14kinds of planted mistake found perfectly. Split orders, the one judgement call: 94%.
8 / 8counts reconcile with the ERP: orders, requests, approvals, receipts, releases, returns, invoices and payments.
